Problem 1: Concepts and fundamental knowledge

(a) VC dimension. The VC dimension is d + 1 for aﬀine binary linear classifiers in Rd , where the appended
constant feature supplies the intercept. The d + 1 aﬀinely independent vertices of a simplex can be shattered.
The standard upper bound for aﬀine halfspaces rules out shattering d + 2 points. The feature count d here
excludes the constant coordinate.
(b) Test error versus out-of-sample error. For a fixed learned classifier h, its out-of-sample classification
error is the population probability

                                      Eout (h) = P(x,y)∼P {h(x) ̸= y}.

The test error is the observed fraction of mistakes on a finite held-out test sample:

                                                   1 X
                                                      m
                                     Etest (h) =       1{h(xj ) ̸= yj }.
                                                   m
                                                     j=1

It varies with the test sample and estimates the population error; with i.i.d. test examples independent of
learning, its expectation conditional on the fixed h equals Eout (h). The two quantities need not be numeri-
cally equal.
(c) Gradient descent: statement 2 is false.
1) True. Convexity gives L(u) ≥ L(θ) + ∇L(θ)⊤ (u − θ). A zero gradient therefore implies L(u) ≥ L(θ)
   for every u.
2) False. For L(θ) = θ2 , one update is θ+ = (1 − 2µ)θ. If µ > 1 and θ ̸= 0, then L(θ+ ) > L(θ).
   Convexity alone cannot control an arbitrarily large step.
3) True. For g = ∇L(θ) ̸= 0, differentiability gives L(θ − αg) = L(θ) − α∥g∥2 + o(α). This decreases
   for suﬀiciently small positive α.
4) True. The iterate displacement is µ∥∇L(θ)∥ and can be tiny because µ is tiny. For the same quadratic,
   an arbitrarily small learning rate can give a tiny displacement far from the minimizer 0.
(d) Softmax: statement 3 is false.
1) True. Exponentiation preserves ordering and all classes share the same positive denominator; the sets
   of maximizers, including ties, coincide.
2) True. Adding c multiplies numerator and denominator by ec , which cancels.
3) False. Scaling scores changes their differences and typically changes the probabilities. For scores (0, 1),
   the second probability is e/(1 + e); after scaling by 2 it is e2 /(1 + e2 ). A positive scale preserves the
   predicted class, but not these probabilities.
4) True. If the true class is y, one-hot cross-entropy reduces to − log qy .

Problem 3(c): Interpretation

Colors and the zero contour. Red means positive mean ρe : the degree-10 model has a larger clean-target
error than the degree-2 model despite having no larger optimal training error. Blue means negative mean
ρe , so degree 10 generalizes better in this particular comparison. White is near zero and the black contour
marks the empirical zero-mean boundary. A negative value does not prove the degree-10 model cannot
overfit relative to another model.
Colors saturate at ±0.25 for visibility. The actual complexity-grid means range from −0.296410 to
1.032363 × 106 , and noise-grid means from −0.146917 to 4.812316 × 106 . These values were not
clipped. Some finite-sample Legendre designs are poorly conditioned: the smallest recorded degree-10
design singular value is 1.304 × 10−5 . Large fitted coeﬀicients can therefore amplify noise or model
misspecification into large population errors, particularly where sample coverage is poor. All trials,
including these extreme ones, contribute to the required arithmetic mean.
Sample count. More observations generally reduce the estimation instability of the flexible model in the
observed grids and allow its approximation advantage to emerge. For example, at Qg = 20, σ 2 = 0.1, the
mean error difference changes from about 968028.958439 at n = 20, to 11.581522 at n = 40, to 2.483317
at n = 60, and to −0.119340 at n = 120. At n = 120, the mean risks are 0.409165 for degree 2 and
0.289825 for degree 10. This is an observed tendency, not a theorem of monotonicity for each sample or
every grid cell.
Noise variance. Noise can be fitted by the extra coeﬀicients of f10 and increase estimation variance. At
Qg = 20, n = 120, the means below move from a degree-10 advantage to a disadvantage as noise increases:

                               σ2              0          0.1           1           2
                          Mean ρe    −0.146917     −0.119340    0.037381    0.196897

Finite paired Monte Carlo estimates need not be monotone at every cell. For example, at n = 40 the zero-
noise mean is 11.665551 and the variance-0.1 mean is 11.581522. Sample-specific cross terms can make
such local comparisons nonmonotone; they are not grounds for discarding trials or changing the prescribed
mean.
Target complexity. Increasing Qg is not always harmful. At σ 2 = 0.1, n = 120, the mean ρe is 0.014199
for Qg = 1, −0.071197 for Qg = 3, −0.296410 for Qg = 10, and −0.119340 for Qg = 20. For targets
of degree at most 2, both model classes contain the target and the extra coeﬀicients mainly add noise-fitting
variance. For intermediate degrees up to 10, the larger model can represent structure that the quadratic omits.
Above degree 10, both models are misspecified; estimation error, omitted high-order components and the
degree-dependent target normalization all matter. Consequently target degree alone does not determine the
relative error.
Can positive relative overfitting occur without observation noise? Yes. The noise-grid mean at σ 2 =
0, Qg = 20, n = 20 is 558740.582041 > 0, whereas at n = 120 it is −0.146917. With degree 20 the target
is outside both fitted model classes, and fitting its values at a limited random set of inputs can induce a poor
degree-10 approximation elsewhere, especially when the design is ill-conditioned. Observation noise is
therefore not necessary. If the noiseless target were contained in the degree-10 class and the design had full
